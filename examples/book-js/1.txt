Cartographer of Small Hours

I. The Ledger

The Municipal Survey Office of Hollin occupied the top two floors of a building that had been a grain exchange, and it still smelled faintly of grain when the heating came on in October. The day staff worked on the upper floor, where the light was good. They mapped the city the way cities are usually mapped: property lines, drains, the widths of streets, the heights of curbs, the positions of lamp posts and fire hydrants and the little iron plates that said where the water could be turned off. Their maps were accurate to the centimeter and were updated every spring.

The lower floor belonged to the night office, which consisted of one room, one desk, one person, and a ledger.

Ines Varga had been that person for eleven years. Before her it had been Dorrit Alm, who had kept the ledger for thirty-four years and had handed it over with the words, "Write what you count. Never write what you expect." Ines had nodded as though she understood, and had understood about a year later, on a night when she had written down fourteen lit windows on Saltgate Street because there were always fourteen, and had then counted, and had found eleven. She had crossed the fourteen out so hard the pen went through the page. The page was still in the ledger. Dorrit, when told about it, had said that it was the most important page in the book.

The ledger was not one book but a shelf of them, forty-six volumes in dark green cloth, each the size of a hymnal. They recorded, night after night, the things the day office did not consider to be features of the city at all. Where the fog came in and in what order it put out the streetlights. Which windows were lit at three in the morning, and for how long, and on which nights of the week. The routes of the bakery vans, the milk floats when there had still been milk floats, the street sweepers, the night buses, the taxis that waited at the same corners for passengers who came or did not come. The paths of foxes. The paths of people who walked at night because they could not sleep, or because they worked, or because walking was the only thing that helped. The sounds: the foghorn at the harbor mouth, the two church clocks that disagreed by forty seconds, the generator behind the fish market, the particular hum that the tram wires made in cold weather and in no other weather.

None of this had ever appeared on a published map. The day office found the night office faintly embarrassing, the way a family is embarrassed by a relative who paints. But the night office had been founded in 1911 by a harbor master who believed that a city which did not know what it did at night did not know itself, and it had survived two wars, three reorganizations, and the arrival of computers, mostly because nobody who had the power to close it had ever read the ledger, and everybody who had read the ledger had lost the wish to close it.

Ines arrived at eleven each evening and left at seven. She spent the first hour reading the previous night's entries and the last hour writing up the current one. Between, she walked.

II. The Method

The method was simple and had not changed since 1911. You chose a route. You walked it at a steady pace, which Dorrit had defined as the pace at which you could count and not get out of breath. You carried a hand counter, a notebook, a pencil, a watch, and a small lantern, which you did not use for seeing but for being seen, because a person walking alone at three in the morning with a notebook is a person who is going to be asked questions, and a person with a lantern is a person who is obviously doing a job.

You counted. Lit windows per block, by floor. Vehicles passing, by type. People, by direction and by whether they were walking, standing, or sitting. Animals, by species, if you could tell. You noted sounds with the time to the minute. You noted the weather every quarter of an hour, and the fog by its own scale, which ran from zero, no fog, to seven, in which the lantern was the only thing you could see and you walked by the sound of your own feet.

What you did not do was interpret. The ledger did not say "a drunk man." It said "one man, standing, Fishers Lane, 02:41, swaying." It did not say "a lovers' quarrel." It said "two people, standing, raised voices, Customs Quay, 01:12 to 01:19, then one east, one west." The interpretation, Dorrit said, was for the morning, and preferably for a different century.

Ines had sixteen routes, each between six and nine kilometers, and she walked one each night in a rotation that put every route on every night of the week over the course of a season. The routes had names that were older than she was. The Quays. The Lamps. Three Bridges. The Long Steps. Saltgate and Back. The Hospital Loop. The route called Nothing, which went through the warehouse district where in Dorrit's time there had been nothing to count, and where now there were studios and a climbing gym and a place that sold coffee at five in the morning to people who had been climbing.

That was the other thing about the ledger. It recorded change without ever noticing it. Dorrit had written "nothing" on the Nothing route four hundred times. Then one night she had written "1 window, lit, 2nd floor, former rope works," and had not remarked upon it, and over the next six years the windows had become twelve, and then forty, and then a street that had a name again. If you read the ledger straight through, the city grew and shrank and shifted like something breathing. If you read a single night, it was a list of small, exact, unremarkable facts.

The instruments were kept in a cupboard that Ines was fairly sure predated the building. Most of them had been made in the office. There was the counter, of course, and a second counter for when you needed to count two things at once. There was the lantern theodolite, which was a lantern with a protractor fixed to its handle, for taking bearings on lit windows from the middle of a bridge. There was the fog board, a square of black card with a grid of white dots, which you held at arm's length to grade fog by how many dots you could still see. There was a tuning fork, for the tram wires. There was a thermometer that had been reading two degrees high since 1974, and a note on it that said so, and a column in the ledger that said "thermometer" rather than "temperature," so that the error was preserved along with the reading.

Ines loved the instruments more than she would have admitted. They were not precise. They were honest. A thing measured with the fog board was a thing measured with the fog board, and anyone who picked up the fog board would measure it the same way, and that was more than could be said of most instruments she had met.

She also loved the walk, which was harder to explain, because it was often cold and sometimes frightening and always lonely, and because she was not by temperament a person who liked being outdoors. The explanation she had settled on, when people asked, was that the city at night was a different city, and that she was the only person who had a map of it. This was true and it was not the reason. The reason was closer to this: that for six hours each night, nothing was required of her except attention, and that attention, practiced that long and that steadily, became a kind of rest.

On the night this account begins, which was a Tuesday in late October, she walked the Quays. The fog was at two when she started and at five by the time she reached the fish market, and she graded it on the board under the market's one working lamp and wrote "5, from the east, lamp 17 gone at 01:50, lamp 16 gone at 01:53." Then she stood for a while in the fog, because there was no point walking when you could not count, and listened to the foghorn, which sounded every ninety seconds and which she did not need to write down because it had been written down eleven thousand times.

A van passed, slowly, headlights making two soft cones. "1 van, bakery (Hallam), west, 01:58," she wrote, by feel. Hallam's van was always west along the quay at two. It went to the hotels first.

Then she heard footsteps, and they were not hers.

III. The Walker

Footsteps at night have a character, and after eleven years Ines could read most of them. Heels going home from a late shift, quick and even. The scuff of someone who has had too much to drink and knows it. The soft, deliberate tread of a person who does not want to be heard, which is the only tread that frightens her, and which she has heard perhaps four times.

These were none of those. They were slow and perfectly regular, and they did not alter when the fog thickened, which meant the person did not need to see where they were going. They came from behind her, along the quay, and passed within a few meters, and she saw a shape, tall, coat, hood up, hands in pockets, and then the fog had it.

"1 person, walking, west, 02:04, steady," she wrote. Then, after a moment, "did not look."

That would have been the end of it. People walked at night. But on Thursday, on the Three Bridges route, which crossed the quay on its way to the second bridge, she heard the same steps at eleven minutes past three, and saw the same shape, going west, and on the following Tuesday, on the Quays again, she heard them at eight minutes past two, and realized she had been waiting for them.

She went back through the ledger. Dorrit had taught her to do this before trusting any pattern, because the ledger was long and the mind was short, and the mind would find patterns in anything. She found the walker on fourteen nights in the past two months, always on routes that touched the quay, always between two and half past three, always going west. Before that, nothing. The walker had started in late August.

She did what the method allowed, which was to count more carefully. She began to vary her routes so that she crossed the quay at the right hour more often, which was a bending of the rotation that Dorrit would have frowned at and then done herself. She noted the exact time and the exact place of each sighting. She began to note the pace, by counting steps against her watch, and found that it was almost exactly one hundred and four steps a minute, every time, in fog or clear.

In three weeks she had a line on the map. It started somewhere east of the fish market, where she had never yet been in time to see it start, and ran the length of the quay, over the first bridge, along the north bank past the old customs house, and ended, as far as she could tell, in the streets behind the hospital, where she had twice lost it among the parked ambulances.

She drew it in pencil on a tracing of the day office's map, and she labeled it, in the private shorthand she used for things that were not yet ready for the ledger, "W." Then she sat looking at it for a long time.

The ledger had a category for people who walked at night without evident purpose, and it was an old category, and its name was not kind. Dorrit had used it. The harbor master who founded the office had used it. Ines had used it herself, in her first years, before she had come to understand that the ledger's power lay in its refusal to say what things were. The category was "sleepwalkers," and it had been meant literally once, in a city where the night was emptier, and it had become a joke, and then a habit, and she had let it stand because the ledger did not like having its categories changed.

She did not write W in that category. She did not write W in the ledger at all, beyond the plain entries of a person walking west. But she thought about the category, and about what it meant that the office had, for over a century, had one word for everyone who was out at night without a van or a uniform or a dog, and that the word assumed they did not know where they were going.

W knew exactly where she was going. The line on the tracing paper was straighter than any route Ines walked herself.

IV. The Planner

Teodor Abeln came to the night office at four in the afternoon, which was the middle of Ines's night, and she met him in her dressing gown at the door of her flat because the message had been marked urgent and she had misread it as a flood.

He was from the planning department. He was polite, tired, and perhaps forty, with the look of a man who had been given a list of things to close and was working through it in alphabetical order. The night office was under N, for Night, or possibly S, for Survey, and either way it had come up.

"I want to be straightforward," he said, in her kitchen, holding a cup of coffee he had not asked for. "The council has a question, and the question is what the night office produces. Not what it does. I understand what it does. I've read the description. The question is what comes out of it that anybody uses."

Ines had expected this conversation for eleven years and had rehearsed it perhaps a thousand times, on the Long Steps, in the fog, and now that it had come she found that every rehearsed answer had left her.

"The ledger," she said.

"The ledger is a record. I'm asking what it's for."

"It's for knowing what the city does at night."

"And who needs to know that?"

She thought about it honestly, which was a mistake, because the honest answer was that she did not know. The day office did not read the ledger. The council did not. The police had asked for it twice in a century, both times about a specific night, and both times the ledger had told them something, a van at an hour it should not have been, a window lit that was supposed to be empty, and both times they had thanked her and not come back. Historians came, occasionally, and left happy and wrote things that she did not read.

"Maps are not for anyone until they're needed," she said finally. "And then they're the only thing that's for anyone. You don't know which ones you'll need. The day office makes the ones everybody agrees you'll need. I make the ones nobody agrees about."

"That's a good line," Teodor said, and she could see that he meant it, and that it would not help. "I need something I can put in a column. A use. A number."

"Then I can't help you."

He finished the coffee, which he had clearly not wanted, and stood, and said that the decision was not his and would not be made before the new year, and that he would come back with the questions in writing. At the door he stopped.

"What's it like?" he asked. "The walking."

"Cold," said Ines.

"Besides that."

She looked past him, down the stairwell, where the afternoon light was coming in at an angle that she almost never saw.

"It's like being the only person awake in a house full of sleepers," she said. "You can hear everything. You can't touch anything. And in the morning nobody believes you."

He wrote that down, which surprised her, in a small notebook he took from his coat, and then he looked embarrassed and put the notebook away and left.

That night she walked the Hospital Loop, which she had not been due to walk until Friday, and at twenty past three she was standing in the shadow of the ambulance bay when W came up the hill from the north bank at one hundred and four steps a minute, and went past her, and went in through the staff entrance, and the door closed, and a light came on three floors up.

"1 person, walking, in, 03:21," Ines wrote. Then she stood for a long time, and then she wrote, "W," and then she wrote, "works here," and then she crossed it out, because she had not counted it. She had only seen it.

V. The Fog

Of all the things the ledger recorded, fog was the one Ines was proudest of, and the one she would have found hardest to defend to Teodor Abeln.

Hollin was a fog city. It sat at the mouth of a river that ran cold out of the hills into a sea that ran warm up from the south, and when the two met in the harbor in autumn, the air went white. Everyone who lived there knew this. What they did not know, because there was no reason for anyone to know it, was that the fog did not arrive. It assembled.

The ledger knew. A century of fog grades, taken every quarter hour on sixteen routes, had given the night office something that no weather station could have given, because weather stations measure at a point and the fog was a shape. Dorrit had begun the fog maps in the sixties, plotting the grades on tracings night by night, and Ines had continued them, and between them they had a picture of the fog that the fishermen had always had in their bodies and nobody had ever had on paper.

It came in layers, and the fishermen had names for the layers. The first was the skim, which lay on the water and did not come ashore. The second was the quay fog, which came ashore and sat in the streets nearest the water and went no further than the first row of buildings. The third was the climber, which went up the hill in the valleys between the streets and put out the lamps one at a time from the bottom, so that if you stood at the top of the Long Steps you could watch the city go out below you like a tide of lights running backward. The fourth had no name because by the time it came there was nobody out to name it.

The order in which the lamps went out was the same every time, within a lamp or two, and Ines had the order written down. Lamp 17 on the quay, then 16, then 14, skipping 15, which stood in a gap between two warehouses where the air moved differently. Then the lamps on the first bridge, from the harbor end. Then Fishers Lane, which filled from the bottom like a bath. Then the customs house lamp, which stood on a corner and lasted longer than it should have because the building behind it held the fog back. She could tell the time by it, on a fog night, more accurately than by the church clocks. She could tell the grade of the fog at the hospital, two kilometers away, by which lamps on the quay were gone.

None of this was a use, in Teodor's sense. Nobody needed to know where the fog was. The ferries had radar and the buses had schedules and the lamps came back on by themselves when the air cleared. The fog map was the purest thing the ledger contained: a hundred years of attention paid to a thing that did not matter, until it was understood so well that it became beautiful, and still did not matter.

She took the tracing of the fog layers to the office one evening in November, when the questions had arrived in writing, and she pinned it to the wall over her desk, and she looked at it while she tried to compose answers. The questions were sensible. What is the annual cost of the night office. What are its outputs. Who are its users. What would be lost if it closed. She answered the first three with numbers and the fourth with a sentence that she rewrote eleven times and finally left as: "A record of what the city does when it believes it is unobserved, kept for one hundred and fourteen years without interruption, which cannot be restarted once stopped."

It was true, and it was the kind of truth that fits in a column only if the column is wide enough, and she doubted that it was.

VI. Maren

The light three floors up in the hospital belonged to the night nurses' room, and the walker's name was Maren Ostrowska, and Ines learned both of these things in the least professional way possible, which was by standing in the ambulance bay for a fourth night and being asked by a paramedic what she wanted.

"I'm from the survey office," she said, and showed the lantern, which was an argument that had worked for a century.

"At three in the morning?"

"That's when I work."

The paramedic, a woman with the build of someone who lifted people for a living, looked at the lantern, and the notebook, and Ines, and seemed to come to a decision that Ines was the kind of strange that is harmless.

"You're watching for Maren," she said.

Ines, who had not known there was a Maren, did not say anything.

"She walks home from the quay every night. From the harbor side. She lives down past the fish market and she walks all the way around by the bridge instead of taking the bus, because she says if she sits down she'll sleep and if she sleeps she'll wake up at the depot. Twelve-hour shifts. Been doing it since the summer, when they cut the night bus." The paramedic shook her head. "Hour and a quarter each way. She says the walking is the only time she's not either working or asleep."

"One hundred and four steps a minute," Ines said, before she could stop herself.

The paramedic looked at her.

"I count," said Ines.

The next week she walked the quay east, against the direction of W, and at ten to two she met her coming out of a side street by the fish market, and fell in beside her, and said that she was from the survey office and that she had been counting Maren for two months and that she thought Maren ought to know.

Maren did not stop walking. She was younger than Ines had thought, under the hood, with the flat, scoured face of someone at the end of a very long day, and she looked at Ines sideways and said, "Counting what?"

"Steps. Times. Where you cross." Ines found the tracing in her coat and held it out, and Maren took it without slowing, and looked at the line that ran from the fish market to the hospital, and at the W, and at the times written along it in pencil.

"That's me," she said.

"Yes."

"You've been following me."

"No. I've been where you were going to be. It's different." Ines heard how it sounded and added, "I'm sorry. The office has a category for people who walk at night. It's a bad category. I wanted a better one."

Maren walked for a while in silence, at one hundred and four steps a minute, and then she said, "What's the bad one?"

"Sleepwalkers."

Maren laughed, which Ines had not expected, a short tired sound that went out into the fog and did not come back.

"That's not wrong," she said. "That's not wrong at all." She gave the tracing back. "What's the better one?"

Ines had thought about this for two months and had not found it. "I don't know yet. Something that says you know where you're going."

"Put 'going home.' It's what I'm doing. It's what most of them are doing, the ones you count. Going home, or going to work, or walking because it's the only time nobody wants anything." She pulled the hood tighter. "You can walk with me if you want. I don't mind. But I'm not going to talk. I've been talking for twelve hours."

So Ines walked with her, to the first bridge and over it and along the north bank, and did not talk, and counted, and at the hospital Maren said "Goodnight" without turning around and went in, and the light came on three floors up.

That night in the ledger, in the column where for a hundred years the office had written "sleepwalker," Ines wrote "going home," and the pen did not go through the page, and she sat looking at the two words for a long time before she went on.

VII. The Surge

The storm came on the second Friday in December, and it came the way the ledger said storms came, which was from the southwest, with the wind rising through the evening and the tide, which was already a spring tide, held up in the harbor by the wind until it had nowhere to go but the streets.

Ines was on the Quays. She should not have been. The day office had sent round a notice at six that evening advising all staff to stay indoors, and she had read it and put on her coat. Dorrit had walked the Quays in the storm of 1983 and had come back with the ledger wrapped in a plastic bag inside her shirt and had written, in a hand that shook, the single most useful page the office had ever produced: the order in which the streets had flooded, to the minute, with the depth at each lamp post.

Nobody had asked for that page in forty years. Ines had read it perhaps twenty times. She knew that the water came up through the drains before it came over the quay. She knew that Fishers Lane filled first because it was lowest and because its drain ran into the same culvert as the fish market's, and the fish market's was always half blocked. She knew that the first bridge's north end was fifteen centimeters lower than its south end, and that the customs house had a cellar door at street level that had not been sealed, and that the hospital's ambulance bay was on the second lowest street in the north bank and nobody had ever thought about it because nobody had ever been there with a notebook when the water came.

She knew all of this because the ledger knew it, and the ledger knew it because for a century people had walked in the rain and written down where the puddles were.

By eleven the quay was awash. By midnight Fishers Lane was knee deep and she was on the first bridge, in the lee of the parapet, writing by the light of the lantern, which was the first time in eleven years she had used it for seeing. "00:04, Fishers Lane, 40 cm at lamp 9. 00:06, customs house, water at cellar door. 00:11, first bridge north end, water over curb." The wind took the top page off the notebook and she let it go.

At twenty past midnight her phone rang, which it had never done at night in eleven years, and it was Teodor Abeln.

"Where are you," he said, without a greeting.

"First bridge."

"Jesus. Get off it."

"It's fine. It's the north end that goes."

A pause, and the sound of a room with a lot of people talking in it. "The emergency room at the hospital says water is coming into the ambulance bay and nobody can tell them why, because the bay isn't on the flood map. The flood map has the river. This isn't the river."

"It's the drains," said Ines. "The bay drains into the north bank culvert. The culvert's backing up from the harbor. It'll be thirty centimeters by one and it'll stop around half past, when the tide turns. Then it'll go down fast. They should move the ambulances to the top of Hill Street. Hill Street doesn't flood. It's never flooded."

"How do you know that?"

"Because I've been standing in it in the rain for eleven years," said Ines, "and Dorrit stood in it for thirty-four before that, and it's in the ledger, and nobody has ever read the ledger."

Another pause. The wind pulled at the parapet.

"Read it to me," Teodor said. "Whatever you've got. Where it goes next."

So she read it to him, from memory and from the notebook, standing on the bridge with the water coming over the north curb exactly as the ledger said it would, and he repeated it to the room, and the room went quiet, and then it got loud in a different way. She told him about Fishers Lane and the customs house cellar and the two streets behind the fish market where the water would stand until morning because their drains ran uphill, which nobody believed until it happened every time. She told him which lamps would go out, and when, because the ledger had the fog order and the flood order and they were almost the same order, because the water and the fog found the same low places. She told him that the first bridge's south end would stay dry and that people could cross it on foot until two, and after that they should not.

At half past one the tide turned and the water in the ambulance bay stopped at thirty-one centimeters, which was wrong by one, and she wrote that down too.

VIII. The Column

The night office was not closed. The decision came in February, in a letter of two paragraphs, the second of which was a request from the planning department for a copy of the flood pages, and a question about whether the fog maps could be digitized, and a sentence that Ines read several times: "The office's records constitute a unique longitudinal dataset of urban night activity with demonstrated operational value."

She pinned it to the wall under the fog tracing. It was the ugliest sentence she had ever been proud of.

Teodor came in March, at eleven at night, which was the right hour, and asked if he could walk with her. He had bought boots. He had a notebook, the same small one he had taken out in her kitchen, and a pencil, and he had the look of a man who had been thinking about something for three months and had decided to stop thinking and go and see.

She gave him the second counter and the fog board and told him the rule.

"Write what you count. Never write what you expect."

"What's the difference?"

"You'll know the first time you get it wrong."

They walked the Quays. The fog was at one and stayed there. He counted lit windows and got the floors wrong and she did not correct him, because the ledger would, in time. At the fish market he said, "What's that?" and she listened and said, "Generator. Since 1998. Write it," and he wrote it. At the first bridge he stopped and looked back at the city, which was doing what it always did at two in the morning, which was almost nothing, slowly.

"It's not what I thought," he said.

"What did you think?"

"I thought it would be empty."

"It is empty. You're counting the empty. That's the job." She pointed down the quay with the lantern. "Van, bakery, west. Write it."

He wrote it. Then he said, "The sentence in the letter. The one about the dataset. I wrote that. I'm sorry about it. It was the only kind of sentence that would fit in the column."

"I know," said Ines. "It's all right. The column's wider than it was."

At ten past two the footsteps came along the quay from the east, one hundred and four to the minute, and Maren went past them in her hood with her hands in her pockets, and lifted one hand without turning her head, and Ines lifted the lantern, and Teodor looked from one to the other and opened his notebook.

"One person," said Ines. "Walking. West. Two twelve." She waited while he wrote it. Then she said, "Going home."

He looked up. "That's not a count."

"No," she said. "It's a category. We changed it."

IX. Spring

The ledger for that year ends, as every volume ends, with the night of the spring equinox, which the harbor master in 1911 had chosen as the boundary between one year's darkness and the next for reasons that nobody had written down and everybody had kept.

Ines walked the Long Steps, because the Long Steps were the route that looked down over everything, and because it was the first clear night in a week. The fog was at zero. From the top she could see every lamp on the quay, 17 and 16 and the gap where 15 stood between the warehouses, and the lights of the first bridge, and the customs house lamp on its corner, and far off across the north bank the hospital with its three floors lit, and the dark bulk of Hill Street above it where the ambulances now parked on stormy nights, by order, in a space that someone from planning had painted with white lines and a small sign.

She counted the lamps. There were two hundred and six, as there had been since 1991. She counted the lit windows, by block and by floor, and got the floors right. She noted the wind, which was from the west and light, and the thermometer, which read two degrees high, and the generator, and the church clocks, which disagreed by forty seconds, and the first bird, which sang at 04:51 from somewhere in the trees behind the customs house, and which she wrote down as "1 bird, unknown, 04:51," because she did not know what kind it was and the ledger did not want her to guess.

At the bottom of the page, where Dorrit had always written the year's last line, she sat for a while with the pencil.

Dorrit's last lines had been plain. "Volume ends. Clear. Nothing further." Forty-six volumes of nothing further, and in between them the whole city, breathing.

Ines wrote: "Volume ends. Clear, from the top of the Long Steps, every lamp visible. One hundred and fourteen years. The city at night is at the following address: wherever someone is awake and counting. Nothing further."

Then she closed the book, and went down the steps in the first gray light, and at the bottom she met the bakery van, west, and lifted the lantern to it, and went home.
